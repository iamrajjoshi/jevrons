## A NAND gate that can't learn

[Mustafa Akın made Jev act as a NAND gate](https://x.com/mustafaakin/status/2103574428475154635) and wired enough of them together to run a 4-bit ALU. Jev is TypeSafe AI's frozen evaluation model, so every gate was an API call. By his numbers, 7 + 5 = 12 took 116 gates, 7.6 seconds and $0.0018, with a compound probability of 84% that the answer came out right. He joked that billions of these will play Doom one day, and had Opus 5.5 cut a Nolan-style video about it. That tweet is why this project exists.

His ALU had no learning in it. Every gate had fixed wiring, and if a gate misfired, the answer was wrong. So the obvious next question was: what if the wiring could learn? If a model acting as a neuron is imperfect, can the weights around it learn to compensate?

Jevrons is my attempt at an answer. It's a small neural network where every neuron is a live Jev call, trained with backprop on handwritten digits. It started as one neuron and a lot of yes/no questions, and each experiment raised the question the next one answered. The result I care about: a network trained with perfect arithmetic falls apart when you drop Jev in, and one trained through Jev doesn't.

## One neuron, one API call

Jev takes a JSON `state` and a set of yes/no questions about it, and for each question returns a Noul: the probability that the "true" criterion holds. There's no logit, no gradient, and no way to fine-tune it[^1].

[^1]: The [API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api) documents answers and token usage, with no gradient or logit output. [Noul](https://docs.typesafe.ai/primitives/noul) is TypeSafe's name for the probability-valued answer.

A normal neuron computes a weighted sum and passes it through an activation. A Jev neuron hands Jev the pieces and asks whether the total is positive:

```text
z = w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + ... + w_k x_k + b      computed in my code, never sent
p = Jev(state)                                 P("the final total is greater than zero")
```

`p` is the neuron's activation and feeds the next layer. The state that ended up working is a flat list of the products `x_i * w_i` for every nonzero input, with the bias appended as one more number. Here's a real hidden neuron from the first training step on digits, trimmed:

```json
{
  "state": {"products": [0.02, -0.11, -0.02, -0.32, 0.36, -0.15, "...", -0.05, 0.0, 0.0]},
  "questions": {
    "fires": {
      "type": "noul",
      "instructions": "Add all the numbers in products together.",
      "criteria": {
        "true": "The final total is greater than zero.",
        "false": "The final total is zero or less."
      }
    }
  }
}
```

That list has 179 numbers. The true total was -5.96, Jev answered `fires: 0.22`, and the neuron correctly stayed off. The call billed 1,282 input tokens and took 0.135 seconds.

Multiplication happens in my code. Adding up and deciding the sign happen inside Jev. Zero pixels are dropped from the state, since they add nothing to the sum, which matters on MNIST where an average image has about 150 nonzero pixels out of 784. Every neuron for every image is its own request, and every request costs money, so a 784-32-10 network is 42 billed calls to classify one digit.

## Does it add?

TypeSafe's own [jaggedness page for Jev 1.13](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness/jev-1.13#math-and-numbers) says it's bad at this. It "does not count reliably", and the error grows with the size of what's being counted. It can't reliably tell whether low-level numeric values are close to each other, its probabilities shouldn't be used to compute exact magnitudes, and the advice is to keep counting and computation in code. Every hidden neuron in a digit network asks it to add about 150 numbers.

So before training anything, I needed to know how badly Jev adds. I built random neuron states from real MNIST pixel values and N(0, 1) weights, with 10 to 250 terms, and asked Jev for the sign. I tried three ways of writing the state: paired `{x, w}` records, parallel `x` and `w` arrays, and the precomputed products. With the bias fixed at zero, 60 neurons per cell:

| Format | 10 terms | 30 | 75 | 150 | 250 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| paired `{x, w}` records | 82% | 87% | 90% | 80% | 77% |
| parallel `x`, `w` arrays | 82% | 85% | 82% | 68% | 65% |
| precomputed products | 93% | 88% | 92% | 82% | 78% |

It adds, roughly. The errors cluster near zero: when the true total is more than one term-spread away from zero, accuracy is 100% up to 75 terms in every format. Products were the most accurate and the cheapest (1,673 tokens at 250 terms versus 5,395 for paired records), so products it was.

"One term-spread" is the useful unit here. A sum of 150 terms that lands at 2.0 is a lot closer to a coin flip than a sum of 10 terms that lands at 2.0, because the bigger list has more room for Jev to lose track. So I measure how far the total is from zero relative to the size of the terms:

```text
spread = sqrt( (x_1 w_1)^2 + (x_2 w_2)^2 + ... + (x_k w_k)^2 )
m      = z / spread                                   normalized margin
```

To see the whole shape, I ran one more probe on the exact format the digit networks use (products with the bias folded in, bias at zero): 600 neurons per term count with margins spread evenly from -3 to 3, each asked twice. That's 6,000 calls for $0.22. Plot the fraction of calls where Jev said "fire" against `m`, one curve per term count:

![Fraction of calls where Jev said fire against the normalized margin m from -3 to 3, one curve per term count, with an exact step at m = 0 dashed. The 10-term curve is steep and crosses 0.5 just right of zero. As the term count grows, the curves get shallower and slide left, until the 250-term curve crosses 0.5 near m = -1.1 and fires on a large share of negative totals.](../figures/jev-margin-by-terms.png)

Each curve fits a shifted normal CDF, with `k` for how sharp it is and `m0` for where it crosses 50/50:

```text
P(fire) ≈ Φ(k · (m - m0))          Φ is the standard normal CDF

terms    k     m0      fires when z < 0   fires when z > 0   sign accuracy
   10   3.2   +0.20          1.9%              91.9%             95.1%
   30   2.2   +0.15          4.8%              92.1%             93.6%
   75   1.8   -0.10          9.5%              96.1%             93.3%
  150   1.8   -0.60         19.4%              98.1%             89.1%
  250   1.5   -1.10         38.8%             100.0%             80.9%
```

That's Jev as a function: a noisy threshold, and two things happen to it as the list gets longer. The curve gets blurrier, with `k` falling from 3.2 to 1.5. More importantly, the threshold slides below zero, so Jev leans toward "fire". At 250 terms, 39% of negative totals fire while every positive total does. Short sums lean slightly the other way. So the long-sum errors are mostly false fires, the same yes-lean that shows up again in the wording trap further down. Asking the same neuron twice flipped the answer 2-5% of the time.

One caveat on this probe: to reach large margins, the sampler nudges all of a neuron's weights the same way, so the high-`|m|` neurons mostly have products of the same sign.

That's the doc's warning, measured. With the bias at zero, accuracy is 82-93% up to 75 terms and 65-82% at 150-250, worst where the total is close to zero. I'm not disproving the page. The network is built to work despite it: the multiplication happens in my code, each neuron only needs the sign of the total, and (as the digit experiments showed) training pushes sums far from zero, where the sign is easy. That error rate is also what I was hoping for. A neuron that's always right would make the experiment pointless, and one at 50% would make it hopeless.

Two other results shaped everything after this.

Jev isn't deterministic. Only 1% of these neurons returned the same probability across 5 identical calls (mean range 0.04, max 0.15), which rules out recording Jev's answers once and training against a lookup table or cache. Every forward pass has to be live.

Neurons also can't share a request. I tried packing 8 neuron states into one request under separate keys, which would have cut the call count 8x. It flipped 11-18% of the answers compared with sending each alone, so it's one neuron per request.

## The bias trick

The first time I included a bias, I wrote it as its own field next to the products:

```text
{"bias": b, "products": [x_1 w_1, x_2 w_2, ..., x_k w_k]}
```

Jev over-trusts it. With the bias drawn independently of the terms, Jev's answer followed the bias sign 72-97% of the time, regardless of what the products added up to. A standalone number next to a long list reads like the headline, and Jev treats it that way.

The fix was to stop giving it a field. Appending the bias to the list as one more product, 40 neurons per cell:

| Format | 10 terms | 30 | 75 | 150 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| products + separate bias | 90% | 85% | 83% | 75% |
| products with bias folded in | 95% | 85% | 85% | 90% |

This looked like a formatting detail. It turned out to decide whether the network could learn XOR at all.

## Backprop without a gradient

Training needs the derivative of each neuron's output with respect to its input, and there are two reasons I can't have it. A threshold's slope is zero everywhere except at the jump, so even a perfect step gives backprop nothing to work with. And Jev is a remote black box: the curves above are something I measured, not something I can differentiate. The standard way around a neuron like this is the straight-through estimator[^2], the same trick used to train quantized and binarized networks. Run the forward pass through the real neuron. In the backward pass, pretend the neuron was a smooth sigmoid of the sum it was supposed to compute.

[^2]: [Bengio, Léonard and Courville 2013](https://arxiv.org/abs/1308.3432) describe straight-through estimators for stochastic and hard-threshold neurons.

The loss is binary cross-entropy on Jev's real output:

```text
L = -[ y log p + (1 - y) log(1 - p) ]
```

For a weight from neuron `i` into neuron `j`, the gradient splits into three factors:

```text
dL/dw_ij ≈ dL/da_j  ·  (1/τ) σ'(z_j / τ)  ·  a_i
           exact       invented               exact
```

The first factor comes from Jev's real outputs downstream, and the last is the real input activation. Only the middle one is made up, a sigmoid's slope standing in for Jev's. It doesn't need to be the right size, only to point the right way, and it does because Jev's firing rate rises with `z` on every curve above.

In code it's a few lines of `net.py`:

```python
acts, zs = forward(params, X, jev_neuron)   # a = Jev's p, z = w·x + b computed in code

for layer in reversed(range(len(params))):
    s = sigmoid(zs[layer] / tau)
    dz = da * s * (1 - s) / tau              # slope of sigmoid(z / tau), not of Jev
    dW, db = acts[layer].T @ dz, dz.sum(0)
    da = dz @ params[layer][0].T             # flows to the earlier layer through w
```

The loss sees Jev's real output, so a misfire costs something. `τ` sets how wide the pretend slope is: 1 on XOR, 0.3 on the circle, and 3 on digits, where the sums are larger. On the digit networks the output layer uses `dL/dz = p - y` directly, as if `p` were a sigmoid, which stays stable when Jev answers close to 0 or 1. Weights are clipped to ±9.99 after every step so they always go out as two-decimal numbers.

The appeal is that it costs no extra calls. The alternative I tested is SPSA, which needs no derivative at all. It nudges every parameter at once by a random ±c and measures the loss twice:

```text
Δ_i ∈ {-1, +1} at random
ĝ_i = [ L(θ + cΔ) - L(θ - cΔ) ] / (2c Δ_i)
    ≈ g_i + Σ_{j≠i} g_j Δ_j / Δ_i
```

That sum is the problem. Each coordinate's estimate carries noise from every other coordinate, so the variance grows with the number of parameters. With a few dozen weights that noise is tolerable in principle. A 784-32-10 network has 25,450, and a few hundred updates of that much noise won't get anywhere.

## XOR

AND and OR need one neuron. XOR needs a hidden layer, so this network had 2 inputs, 4 hidden Jev neurons and 1 output Jev neuron, which is 20 calls to run the four rows of the truth table once. I trained three versions with the same call budget: straight-through with the bias as its own field, straight-through with the bias folded in, and SPSA. Each trained network then ran 5 more times through live Jev. The whole run took 33,183 calls and $0.46, including a first attempt that an HTTP 520 killed.

| XOR | Seed 0 | Seed 1 | Seed 2 | Fully correct passes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| straight-through, separate bias | 75% | 50% | 70% | 0/15 |
| straight-through, folded bias | 95% | 100% | 100% | 14/15 |
| SPSA | 75% | 65% | 80% | 3/15 |

Same network, same rule, same budget. Moving the bias into the list took XOR from zero solved seeds to three. SPSA lost even here, and lost on AND too (5 of 15 fully correct passes, against 15 and 13 for the two straight-through versions). They tied on OR. SPSA didn't come with me to digits.

Two runs were more interesting than the table. Seed 0 with the folded bias works on live Jev (4 of 5 passes perfect), but its weights fail XOR when I run them through an exact step neuron. It learned a solution for how Jev actually behaves, one that's wrong under ideal arithmetic. Seed 2 with the separate bias is the reverse: its weights solve XOR with an exact neuron, and Jev running them gets 70%. A correct design, broken by how the neuron reads the bias.

Those two runs suggested an experiment. Train one network through Jev and an identical one with an exact neuron, then run both on Jev.

## A circle

The circle is the classic toy: points in a square, labeled by whether they fall inside a circle of radius 0.8. The network is 2 inputs, 6 hidden Jev neurons and 1 output Jev neuron, trained for 150 steps on 250 points and tested on 250 others. It cost $1.16 over 83,748 calls.

For each seed I trained a second network from the same starting weights with an exact step neuron, locally and for free, and then swapped Jev in:

| Seed | Trained through Jev, run on Jev | Trained exact, run exact | Trained exact, run on Jev |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 84.2% | 93.2% | 47.2% |
| 1 | 86.2% | 90.0% | 47.2% |
| 2 | 88.4% | 81.2% | 70.2% |

A linear classifier gets 62.4% on the same points. The exact-trained networks are fine with an exact neuron and fall to or below linear once Jev runs them. The Jev-trained ones hold 84-88%.

![Two heatmaps of Jev's output probability over the square from -1 to 1, with the true circle dashed. Left, trained through Jev: a red region fills most of the circle, held-out accuracy 84%. Right, trained with an exact neuron and run on Jev: blue everywhere, held-out accuracy 47%.](../figures/stage3-boundary.png)

The right panel is the failure in one picture. The exact-trained network on Jev never pushes its output above 0.5 anywhere, so it calls every point outside. The left boundary is blocky and lopsided, but it's a boundary.

The Jev-trained weights also scored lower on an exact neuron (77-87%) than on Jev itself. They didn't learn the ideal function and put up with the noise. They learned something fitted to Jev.

## Digits with the sum in code

Before letting Jev add up 150 pixels, I wanted the opposite extreme: my code computes `z`, and Jev only answers whether that one number is positive.

That's where the wording trap showed up. When I first swept 2,181 values of `z` from -100 to 100, the question was phrased as a statement: "z is a number." as the instructions, "z is greater than zero." and "z is zero or less." as the two criteria. Jev returned 0.66-0.74 for every negative `z`. It was a clean step, but with the "no" side sitting above 0.5, so a thresholded neuron said yes to everything. Rewording it as a question, "Is the number z positive?", gave 0.01-0.05 for `z <= 0` and 0.99 for `z >= 0.1`, correct on every value within ±10.

With that question, I trained a 784-32-10 network locally with an exact step neuron and then replaced every neuron with a Jev call (84,000 calls, $1.10):

| 1,000 images | Exact step | Jev activation | Hidden fires as intended | Output fires as intended |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Validation | 77.5% | 77.0% | 99.95% | 99.89% |
| Test | 78.5% | 79.1% | 99.96% | 99.92% |

Handed one number, Jev is a step function. It fired as intended on 99.96% of hidden evaluations. That makes this network the control: there's nothing for training to compensate for.

It also caught a mistake in my local baseline. I'd measured ~83-84% for a hard-step 784-32-10 network on 1,000 images. With near-binary outputs, 11-22% of images have no output neuron firing, and I'd been breaking those ties with the exact output sums, which sneaks perfect arithmetic into the prediction. With random tie-breaking the honest ceiling is about 77-79%, and every digit result here uses random tie-breaking.

## Digits with Jev doing the sum

Next, the real thing on a smaller problem: a 784-32-1 network where every neuron, hidden and output, gets the full products list. Hidden neurons add about 150 products each, and the output neuron adds 33 (32 hidden activations times their weights, plus the bias). Two pairs, 0 vs 1 and 3 vs 8, one seed each, 500 training and 500 validation images, 5 epochs. It took 297,000 calls and $13.88, most of the money spent on the project so far.

Before spending anything, I tuned the settings against a local mock neuron fitted to the error curve from the adding test. It predicted 93.6% on 3 vs 8 when trained through the neuron and 76.5% when swapped.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 vs 1 | trained through Jev | 99.2% | 99.4% | 99.4% | 99.6% |
| 0 vs 1 | trained exact, swapped | 99.4% | 91.5% | 96.4% | 84.5% |
| 3 vs 8 | trained through Jev | 94.4% | 94.2% | 93.0% | 97.5% |
| 3 vs 8 | trained exact, swapped | 92.8% | 67.8% | 86.9% | 91.6% |

![Bar chart of validation accuracy on 500 images. For 0 vs 1, the Jev-trained net gets 99% on both the exact neuron and live Jev, and the exact-trained net gets 99% exact but 92% on live Jev. ## Two ways the weights adapt

The "fires as intended" columns explain the gap. On random 150-term states, Jev gets the sign right 80-90% of the time. The Jev-trained hidden neurons get it right 93-99% of the time. Same model, same term count, same wording. Training changed which sums Jev gets asked about.

The curves from the adding test say why. Because the forward pass is live, what gradient descent actually minimizes is the loss averaged over how Jev really behaves:

```text
min over W, b of   E[ L ]      with each neuron firing with probability ≈ Φ(k · (z / spread - m0))
```

There are two things to beat: the noise, and a yes-lean that grows with the length of the sum. The obvious way to lower the expected loss is to make `|z|` large relative to `spread`, so every neuron sits out on the flat parts of its curve where Jev is almost never wrong. That's margin maximization, the same idea behind support vector machines, arrived at without anyone asking for it. The swapped networks were never under that pressure. An exact neuron fires the same way at 0.01 and at 40 and has no lean, so their sums sit wherever an ideal step is happy, often near zero, where Jev is close to a coin flip. With identical architecture and inputs, their hidden neurons fire as intended 87-96% of the time.

You can watch it happen. Going back through the journaled training calls, I tracked how many hidden sums sat within one spread of zero (`|m| < 1`) each epoch, replaying the exact-trained twins locally from the same start:

![Four panels of hidden-neuron margin over training epochs, for the ten-digit seeds 0 and 1 and the two-digit pairs 0 vs 1 and 3 vs 8. In every panel the median margin of the network trained through Jev rises above that of the exact-trained twin, and in the lower row its hidden neurons fire as intended more often.](../figures/stage6b-margin-dynamics.png)

On 3 vs 8, the share of hidden sums with `|m| < 1` fell from 32% to 13% when training through Jev, and only from 49% to 39% when training exact. On 0 vs 1 it went from 31% to 2%, against 36% to 18%. The margin curve alone predicts the exact twins' live fires-as-intended rate to within 2.4 points.

The weights show it too. Both 3 vs 8 networks started from the same random weights:

![First-layer weights of the 32 hidden neurons on 3 vs 8, drawn as 28 by 28 maps. The random start and the exact-trained weights both look like noise. The weights trained through Jev show faint digit-shaped structure, and their change from the start draws clear 3 and 8 templates, larger and smoother than the exact-trained change.](../figures/stage6b-weights-s5-3v8.png)

Training through Jev moved the first layer more than twice as far (a change of 42.3 against 18.0, in Frobenius norm), and the change draws clear 3 and 8 templates, marking the gaps on the left of a 3 where an 8 has ink. The exact-trained weights still look like the random start.

One prediction didn't hold. If training were compensating for Jev's yes-lean, the Jev-trained sums should drift negative. They drift the other way: on ten digits, the median neuron's mean sum moved +4.1 when trained through Jev and -3.5 when trained exact. Training doesn't learn to counter the lean. It pushes sums far enough out that the lean matters less. My best guess at why the Jev-trained weights move so much further is that Jev's `p` almost never reaches 0 or 1, so the output error `p - y` never vanishes and every example keeps pushing, the way logistic regression does, while an exact step stops learning from anything it already gets right. That's plausible and untested.

The second thing training does is tolerate the misfires that remain. Even at 93% on 3 vs 8, Jev is wrong on 7% of hidden evaluations, and the network still gets 94.2%. It learned weights whose downstream use absorbs those errors. The local mock neuron I tuned against didn't capture how far real training moves the sums, which is why it underestimated the swap gap.

That answers the question Mustafa's ALU raised for me, at least for two digits. The weights can compensate, and they do it both by steering away from the inputs the neuron is bad at and by absorbing the errors it still makes.

## Quirks I didn't know to look for

The bias turned out to be the first of several quirks, and I didn't find it by reading Jev's docs. The first probe set the bias to cancel the sum, and when the bias sign disagreed with the true total, Jev followed the bias 64% of the time. That was a surprise, and XOR proved it mattered: 0 of 3 seeds solved with a separate bias, 3 of 3 with it folded in. Probing and optimization both run straight into whatever the neuron actually does. With about 1.47 million journaled neuron calls, each with a known true sign, I went looking for the rest.

A logistic model of Jev's answer, fitted on the margin plus a few features of the list, picks out three things beyond the length blur and yes-lean from the curves above. Jev counts signs: at 120-199 terms, a list where 60% of the terms are positive gets pushed toward "fire" about as much as moving the total 0.6 spreads. It gives the first third of the list slightly more weight than the last third (5-24% more). And it isn't skimming. When a partial sum of the first few terms has the wrong sign, Jev sides with the full total most of the time. None of these beat the margin, though. Adding them improves agreement with Jev's answers by only 0.1-0.4 points. This describes what moves Jev. It isn't a model of how Jev computes.

To check the correlations, I built trap states, each paired with a control that has the same term count, total and spread, 200 of each, for 1,800 calls and $0.075:

![Misfire rates for five trap families against matched controls, 200 states per bar. One big positive number mid-list misfires 82% of the time, against 28% when it's first and 4% when it's last. A sign-vote trap misfires 99% against 59% for its control. A 250-term list at m = -1 misfires 74% against 0% for its mirror image. Zero padding makes no difference.](../figures/stage6b-traps.png)

Three traps worked. A list of 60 small positives and 15 large negatives, with a negative total, fires 99% of the time against 59% for its control. A 250-term list one spread below zero fires 74.5% of the time, and its mirror image at one spread above zero never fails. And one big positive number buried in the middle of 74 small negatives fools Jev 82.5% of the time, against 27.5% as the first term and 4.5% as the last. I'd predicted the last slot would be the most misleading. Padding a list with 110 zeros did nothing.

Jev's `p` also isn't a probability in the calibrated sense. When Jev says 0.75-0.85, the total is positive 94-99% of the time, not 80%, and across every call its expected calibration error is 0.154. Its answers are squeezed toward the middle, so near either end Jev is more often right than its `p` suggests.

## $17 of API calls

Everything before the ten-digit run came to $17.40 over about 532,000 calls. Jev bills $0.042 per million input tokens, so even the two-digit networks, at 330.5 million input tokens, were $13.88. Money was never the constraint. The number of calls was: at 42 requests per image, one pass over 1,000 images is 42,000 of them.

## One seed, 500 images

The two-digit results are one seed per network, on 500 validation images, and I've only reported validation, not test. The 8- and 26-point gaps are single runs. The ten-digit run is where that gets repeated.

The circle networks weren't converged (150 steps of batch 16), and exact-trained networks varied a lot by seed with those settings when I ran them locally (47-86% across 10 seeds). The swap gap pointed the same way on all three seeds, but its size per seed is noisy.

I don't know how correlated Jev's errors are across neurons in the same image, which both mocks assumed away. I haven't tried the dense 784-pixel version or compared soft versus sampled activations. And the 84% in the opening is his reported compound figure for the ALU, not something Jevrons measured.

## A digit you can draw

The claim I want to be able to make is simple: training through Jev recovers accuracy that swapping Jev in loses. Two pairs of digits show it. All ten digits across three seeds will either show it or not.

After that comes a bigger training set, 10,000 images with the best ten-digit configuration and a final test pass. And I'm building a live draw-a-digit page where each drawing runs through the network on real Jev calls, showing each neuron's intended sum, Jev's answer, and where the two disagree. Those disagreements are the whole point. Without them it would look like any small MLP.
